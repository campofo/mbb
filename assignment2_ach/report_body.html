1. Key judgment

The resigning scientist deliberately took Aurora's intellectual property (high confidence), so H4 is rejected. Separately, the remote-access intrusion is more likely state-linked espionage, the technical part of H1, than the work of cybercriminals (moderate confidence). Whether that state-linked actor directed the scientist is the most consequential open question, and I can answer it only with low confidence: the two activities look concurrent rather than coordinated, which makes an insider acting alone (H2) or for a competitor (H5) somewhat more likely than a fully coordinated H1 operation.

2. Hypothesis set

  • H1, state-linked economic espionage: a state-linked or state-tolerated actor is acquiring Aurora's IP for its state's pharmaceutical sector, by intrusion, a co-opted insider, or both.
  • H2, independent insider: the scientist, acting on her own initiative, is taking data she believes will help her at her new employer.
  • H3, financially motivated cybercriminals: a criminal group got in through the phishing to steal data for extortion or resale, and the resignation is coincidental.
  • H4, benign or false alarm: each signal has an innocent explanation and no IP was stolen.
  • H5 (added), competitor-directed commercial espionage: the rival firm, acting for its own commercial benefit and without state direction, induced or tasked the scientist during her recruitment to bring specific Aurora data, including manufacturing and supply-chain information. The remote-access intrusion is unrelated.

H5 changes who decides what is taken. In H2 the scientist decides; in H5 a private company does, for corporate goals, and in H1 a state does. That difference produces predictions of its own: contact with the rival before the formal offer, collection matched to the rival's commercial gaps, and no connection to the APT infrastructure.

3. Evidence and diagnosticity

The matrix splits into an insider cluster (items 1, 4, 7, 8 and 9) and a technical cluster (items 2, 3, 6, 10 and 11), with item 5 bearing on both. Few items discriminate at all. Most rows are Consistent with three or four hypotheses and so confirm almost nothing, which leaves three items doing most of the work.

Item 6, the certificate overlap, is the most diagnostic item for the technical cluster. Without it, items 2 and 3 fit H1, H3 and even H4, since an unapproved remote tool can be shadow IT and a login from a new device can be a new phone. Item 6 is the only evidence about who operates the tool: its command servers use a certificate seen before on infrastructure that another vendor attributed to an APT group with a documented history of targeting pharmaceutical and biotech IP. That makes it Inconsistent with both a criminal operator and an innocent explanation. The weakness is that H1's technical claim rests almost entirely on this one match, attributed second-hand and with moderate confidence. Certificates and hosting are often shared between actors and are sometimes planted as false flags. Because so much depends on one item, my judgment on the technical cluster is moderate rather than high.

Item 9, the 60/40 file mix, does the same job for the insider cluster. A personal backup would contain her own project's files. Forty percent of the download came from a different drug candidate, and those files were manufacturing-cost and supply-chain records, which have little to do with her research. Item 9 is therefore decisively Inconsistent with H4. It is also the only evidence about what the collector wanted. A scientist advancing her own career would value her own research, the 60%. Cost and supply-chain data matter to a business competing with Aurora, or to a state supporting such businesses, so item 9 fits H1 and H5 more naturally than H2, which accommodates it only through its "useful at her new employer" wording. Item 8 limits how far this goes: she reached those files through an accidental ACL fault, and grabbing whatever happened to be reachable explains the mix as well as a shopping list would.

The third discriminator is item 4, the personal cloud upload, together with my derived row E12. Criminals send stolen data to their own infrastructure, not to an employee's named account, so item 4 counts against H3. It also shows that the two channels diverge. If the actor running the RAT were also running her, it already had a covert channel inside her division, and having its asset upload through a traceable personal account that DLP flagged would be poor tradecraft for a state service. Handlers do sometimes keep insiders and implants apart on purpose, and inexperienced assets make mistakes, so the point is not conclusive. It is still the only evidence that bears directly on coordination, and it points away from it.

Item 7 is the item that feels most incriminating, a rival firm in the same region as the IP addresses, yet it is Consistent with H1, H2, H4 and H5. Resigning to join a rival is common, and "same general region" is too vague to tie the firm to item 2. Items 1, 8 and 10 are similarly Consistent with almost everything. Item 11 lowers the weight of item 3: the phishing was part of a regional wave across the sector, so Aurora was probably not singled out. That fits an APT and criminals equally, and it makes the closeness in time between the phishing and the resignation easier to accept as coincidence. Item 5 counts only against H3, and it is absence of evidence over a short window.

4. Inconsistencies and what they rule out (or don't)

The tallies are H1 = 1, H2 = 0, H3 = 4, H4 = 3 and H5 = 0. Read literally, they put H2 and H5 in front, but that follows from how the counting works. Both hypotheses explain only the insider cluster and are rated NA on items 2, 3, 6 and 11 because they say nothing about the RAT. Their score of zero means only that nothing in the matrix contradicts them; any complete account built on either one still needs a second actor for the intrusion. H1's low count partly reflects its breadth, because an actor using "intrusion, an insider, or both" is hard to contradict. Its one inconsistency, E12, falls on the coordinated-insider version, which is the version that would justify treating the scientist as a state agent.

H4 has fewer inconsistencies than H3 and is still the more firmly disqualified, because its three are independent and heavy. Item 3's credential reuse could just about be a new device, and item 6 could just about be a mistaken attribution. No backup habit explains item 9, and that item alone disqualifies H4 for the insider cluster. A benign reading of the technical cluster survives only if item 6 collapses and the unseen device also turns out to be innocent, which I judge unlikely.

H3's four inconsistencies (items 4, 5, 6 and 10) are softer one by one. Criminals sometimes sit on data or sell it quietly, which weakens item 5. A footprint as small as item 10 describes is unusual for them but possible, and criminals do share infrastructure, which weakens item 6. Together they still add up, and nothing in the matrix favors H3 over H1, since items 2, 3 and 11 fit both equally. H3 is unlikely but not excluded. If item 6 fails, it becomes the leading alternative for the technical cluster.

5. Conclusion, confidence, and what would change my mind

My final judgment is compound. I am highly confident that the scientist deliberately removed Aurora IP, including data outside her own project. The RAT activity is, with moderate confidence, state-linked espionage rather than crime. On whether the two are coordinated my confidence is low: an independent insider (H2) or a competitor-directed one (H5) is marginally favored over a coordinated H1, and no current evidence separates H2 from H5. The evidence neither shows that she is a state agent nor rules out a connection between the two activities. It runs out at what the phished account and the RAT accessed, which the scenario does not report.

The single most consequential new fact would be any forensic link between the RAT and the resigning scientist: the tool running on her workstation, her credentials passing through it, or her downloaded files appearing in its traffic. If such a link exists, coordinated H1 becomes the leading hypothesis at moderate-to-high confidence and the case becomes a state-espionage counterintelligence matter. If the RAT host turns out to be the phished scientist's machine and shows no contact with her data, the judgment that the activities are separate gets stronger. A close second is item 6 itself. If its attribution is withdrawn, or the certificate proves to be shared commodity hosting, the technical cluster drops to low confidence between H1 and H3.

I would monitor four indicators next:

  • Further connections to the item-2 IP ranges or the matched certificate fingerprint, shared through the ISAC. If other members see the same infrastructure, the campaign is sector-wide rather than aimed at Aurora.
  • Repository logs for the phished account and the roughly 40 users exposed by the ACL fault, to establish exactly what the intruder reached.
  • Leak sites and criminal marketplaces for the next 60 to 90 days. A late extortion demand would revive H3.
  • The rival firm's patent filings, trial registrations, and manufacturing or supplier changes that resemble the second candidate's cost and supply-chain data, along with any sign that it contacted the scientist before the offer. Either would favor H5 or H1 over H2, since H2 does not predict quick corporate use of commercial data.
