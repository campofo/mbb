1. Key judgment

The evidence best supports a compound explanation: the resigning scientist deliberately stole intellectual property (high confidence, which rejects H4), while a separate remote-access intrusion is more likely state-linked espionage, the technical part of H1, than cybercrime (moderate confidence). The question that matters most, whether that actor directed the insider, I can answer only with low confidence. On current evidence the two activities look concurrent rather than coordinated, so an insider acting alone (H2) or for a competitor (H5) is somewhat more likely than a fully coordinated H1 operation.

2. Hypothesis set

  • H1, state-linked economic espionage: a state-linked or state-tolerated actor is acquiring Aurora's IP for its state's pharmaceutical sector, by intrusion, a co-opted insider, or both.
  • H2, independent insider: the scientist, acting on her own initiative, is taking data she believes will help her at her new employer.
  • H3, financially motivated cybercriminals: a criminal group got in through the phishing to steal data for extortion or resale. The resignation is coincidental.
  • H4, benign or false alarm: each signal has an innocent explanation and no IP was stolen.
  • H5 (added), competitor-directed commercial espionage: the rival firm, acting for its own commercial benefit and without state direction, induced or tasked the scientist during her recruitment to bring specific Aurora data, including manufacturing and supply-chain information. The remote-access intrusion is unrelated.

H5 is genuinely distinct because it changes who decides what is taken. Unlike H2, the collection is directed from outside. Unlike H1, the director is a private company with corporate rather than state goals. It therefore predicts different things: contact with the rival before the formal offer, collection matched to the rival's commercial gaps, and no link to the APT infrastructure.

3. Evidence and diagnosticity

The matrix splits into two clusters. The insider cluster is items 1, 4, 7, 8 and 9. The technical cluster is items 2, 3, 6, 10 and 11. Item 5 bears on both. The most important pattern is how few items discriminate at all. Most rows are Consistent with three or four hypotheses, so they confirm almost nothing. Three items do most of the analytic work.

Item 6 (C2 certificate overlap) is the most diagnostic item for the technical cluster. Without it, items 2 and 3 fit H1, H3, and even H4: an unapproved remote tool can be shadow IT, and a login from a new device can be a new phone. Item 6 is the only evidence about who operates the tool. It links the tool to an actor known for stealing biotech IP, which makes it Inconsistent with both a criminal operator and an innocent explanation. Its weakness is that H1's technical claim rests almost entirely on it. It is a single certificate match, attributed second-hand and with moderate confidence by a different vendor, and certificates and hosting get reused, shared, and occasionally planted as false flags. That single point of failure is why my judgment on the technical cluster is moderate rather than high.

Item 9 (60/40 file mix) is the most diagnostic item for the insider cluster. A personal backup would contain her own project's files. Forty percent of the download belonged to a different drug candidate, and those files were manufacturing-cost and supply-chain records: commercial information, not scientific. That makes item 9 decisively Inconsistent with H4. It is also the only evidence about what the collector actually wanted. A scientist advancing her own career would value her own research, the 60%. Cost and supply-chain data matter to a business competing with Aurora, or to a state supporting such businesses. So item 9 fits H2 only loosely, through H2's "useful at her new employer" wording, and fits H1 and H5 more naturally. There is an important caveat from item 8: she reached those files through an accidental ACL fault. Grabbing whatever happened to be reachable is a real alternative to a shopping list. Item 9 rules out H4 and tilts toward directed collection without settling the question.

Item 4 (personal cloud upload), together with my derived row E12, is the third discriminator. Criminals send stolen data to their own infrastructure, not to an employee's named account, so item 4 counts against H3. More importantly, it shows the two channels diverging. If the actor running the RAT were also running her, it already had a covert channel inside her division. Having its asset upload through a traceable personal account that DLP flagged would be poor tradecraft for a state service. This is not conclusive: handlers sometimes keep insiders and implants separate on purpose, and inexperienced assets make mistakes. But it is the only evidence that bears directly on coordination, and it points away from it.

Several items are weak despite how they look. Item 7 feels like the most incriminating fact, a rival firm in the same region as the IP addresses, yet it is Consistent with H1, H2, H4 and H5. Resigning to join a rival is ordinary, and "same general region" is too vague to tie the firm to item 2. Items 1, 8 and 10 are similarly Consistent with almost everything. Item 11 actually lowers the weight of item 3: the phishing was part of a regional wave across the sector, so Aurora was probably not singled out. That fits an APT or criminals equally, and it makes the closeness in time between the phishing and the resignation more plausibly coincidental. Item 5 discriminates only against H3, and it is absence of evidence over a short window.

4. Inconsistencies and what they rule out (or don't)

The tallies are H1 = 1, H2 = 0, H3 = 4, H4 = 3, H5 = 0. Read literally, H2 and H5 lead. That is an artifact of the method. Both explain only the insider cluster and are rated NA on items 2, 3, 6 and 11 because they say nothing about the RAT. Zero inconsistencies means "nothing here contradicts it," not "it explains everything." Any complete account built on H2 or H5 still needs a second actor for the intrusion. H1's low count partly reflects how broad it is: an actor using "intrusion, an insider, or both" is hard to contradict. Its one inconsistency (E12) falls on the coordinated-insider version specifically, which is the version that would justify treating the scientist as a state agent.

H4 has fewer inconsistencies than H3 but is the more firmly disqualified, because its three inconsistencies are independent and carry heavy weight. Item 3's credential reuse could just about be explained away as a new device. Item 6 could just about be a mistaken attribution. Item 9, however, cannot be explained by a backup habit, and on its own it disqualifies H4 for the insider cluster. A benign reading of the technical cluster survives only if item 6 collapses and the unseen device turns out to be innocent, and I judge that unlikely.

H3's four inconsistencies (items 4, 5, 6 and 10) are each softer. Criminals sometimes sit on data or sell it quietly, so item 5 is weak. A small footprint is unusual for them but not impossible (item 10). Item 6 is a moderate-confidence attribution, and criminals do share infrastructure. Taken together, though, they add up, and no item favors H3 over H1: items 2, 3 and 11 fit both equally. H3 is therefore unlikely but not excluded. It is the leading alternative for the technical cluster if item 6 fails.

5. Conclusion, confidence, and what would change my mind

My final judgment is a compound one. With high confidence, the scientist deliberately removed Aurora IP, including data outside her own project. With moderate confidence, the RAT activity is state-linked espionage rather than criminal. With low confidence, the two are not coordinated. An independent insider (H2) or a competitor-directed one (H5) is marginally favored over a coordinated H1, and no current evidence separates H2 from H5. I am not claiming that she is a state agent, and I am not claiming that the events are definitely unrelated. The evidence runs out at what the phished account and the RAT actually accessed, and nothing in the scenario reports that.

Sensitivity. The single most consequential new fact would be any forensic link between the RAT and the resigning scientist. That could be the tool running on her own workstation, her credentials being used through it, or the files she downloaded appearing in its traffic. If such a link exists, coordinated H1 becomes the leading hypothesis at moderate-to-high confidence and the case becomes a state-espionage counterintelligence matter. If the RAT host turns out to belong to the phished scientist and shows no contact with her data, my judgment that the activities are concurrent but separate grows stronger. A close second: if item 6's attribution is withdrawn, or the certificate proves to be shared commodity hosting, the technical cluster falls to low confidence between H1 and H3.

Indicators to monitor.

  • Infrastructure: any further connections to the item-2 IP ranges or the matched certificate fingerprint, shared through the ISAC. If other members see the same infrastructure, that points to a sector-wide APT campaign rather than one aimed at Aurora.
  • Access logs: repository logs for the phished account and the roughly 40 ACL-exposed users, to establish exactly what the intruder reached.
  • Criminal markets: continued leak-site and marketplace monitoring for 60–90 days. A delayed extortion demand would revive H3.
  • The rival firm: patent filings, trial registrations, or manufacturing and supplier changes that resemble the second candidate's cost and supply-chain data, plus any evidence of contact with the scientist before the offer. Either would favor H5 or H1 over H2, which does not predict quick corporate use of commercial data.
